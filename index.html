British India: Governors, Governor-Generals & Viceroys

A corrected, exam-oriented chronological guide — offices, appointments, policies, resistance, national movement and economy.

Choose a language. The detailed chronology tables are currently presented in English; the Bengali interface and key explanatory notes are available.

How to use this guide

This resource separates offices that are often mistakenly mixed together. It includes the main chronological officeholders and major acting/interim holders where they matter. Individual dates can differ slightly between sources because some record appointment, arrival or assumption of charge.

Important correction: Robert Clive was Governor of Bengal in 1757–1760 and again from 1765; Warren Hastings became Governor of Bengal in 1772 and Governor-General of Bengal under the Regulating Act in 1773/1774. William Bentinck became the first Governor-General of India under the Charter Act of 1833 (the new title took effect in 1834). Canning became the first Viceroy when Crown rule began in 1858. Mountbatten was the last Viceroy; C. Rajagopalachari was the first and only Indian Governor-General after independence.

Historical interpretation is identified as interpretation. For example, the effects of colonial trade, land revenue, famine policy and “drain of wealth” are discussed by historians; numerical estimates and regional effects are not always agreed upon.

Terminology: “Governor of Bengal” before 1773 is not the same office as “Governor-General of Bengal.” Likewise, the Governor of Bengal province after 1912 was a provincial office, not the Viceroy.

How appointments worked

  • Company period: senior officials were appointed through the East India Company’s corporate and political structures. After the Regulating Act (1773) and Pitt’s India Act (1784), Parliament and the British government exercised stronger oversight.
  • Crown period: the Crown appointed the Governor-General/Viceroy on the advice of British ministers. The Viceroy governed through an executive council and provincial administrations; London retained ultimate imperial authority.
  • Not elected by Indians: these senior colonial officeholders were not chosen by a popular Indian vote. Limited Indian representation in councils and legislatures expanded over time, but did not amount to full responsible government.

Why the Company’s power expanded

  • 1757 — Plassey: Clive defeated Siraj ud-Daulah with support from conspirators within the Nawab’s camp.
  • 1764 — Buxar: Company forces defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah and Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.
  • 1765 — Diwani: the Mughal emperor granted revenue-collection rights in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the Company.
  • 1772 — Dual Government ended: Hastings brought revenue and administration more directly under Company control.

Administration, economy and resistance

The Company used Indian revenue to finance its army, administration and purchases of Indian goods. The Bengal famine of 1770 was a severe humanitarian disaster. It reflected drought alongside structural agrarian vulnerability and failures in revenue and relief administration. Historians debate the relative weight of these factors.

Resistance in this era was regional rather than a unified national movement: Fakir–Sannyasi resistance in Bengal (associated with figures such as Majnu Shah), Mysore’s wars under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, and Maratha resistance to British expansion. Raja Rammohan Roy belongs to the early nineteenth-century reform and intellectual sphere, not to the later mass nationalist movement.

Governors of Bengal, Madras and Bombay: separate presidency offices

“Governor” was a local administrative title. The presidency governors managed settlements, revenues, armies and regional administration. As the Company expanded, their freedom of action was increasingly constrained by the Governor-General and central authorities.

What did presidency governors actually do?

  • Managed local administration, revenue collection, trade, ports and Company troops.
  • Negotiated with Indian rulers and implemented Company or Crown policy within the presidency.
  • Presidency systems differed: Bengal’s Permanent Settlement (1793) is associated with Cornwallis; ryotwari administration in Madras is associated with Thomas Munro; Bombay developed its own revenue arrangements.
  • They did not independently direct all-India policy once central authority became dominant.

Governor-General of Bengal (1773/1774–1833)

The Regulating Act of 1773 created the office of Governor-General of the Presidency of Fort William in Bengal, supported by a council. Warren Hastings was the first; official chronologies sometimes date the formal assumption of the new title to 1774. The Governor-General’s supervisory role over Madras and Bombay grew over time.

Economy and resistance, 1773–1833

Revenue extraction financed Company administration, armies and commercial purchases. Permanent Settlement fixed the state demand on zamindars in Bengal but did not guarantee secure or prosperous lives for cultivators. The Subsidiary Alliance and wars expanded territorial control and generated costs. Resistance remained largely regional: Mysore under Tipu Sultan, Maratha confederacies, Nepal, and local agrarian or religious movements. A unified all-India national movement had not yet emerged.

Governor-General of India (1833/1834–1858)

The Charter Act of 1833 redesignated the central office as Governor-General of India; the new arrangement took effect in 1834. Bentinck was the first to hold the new title. The office became more explicitly all-India in legislative scope.

The revolt began among sepoys at Meerut in May 1857 and spread to multiple regions. It brought together soldiers, dispossessed rulers, peasants and other groups, but their motives and objectives differed. Calling it a major anti-colonial uprising is reasonable; describing it as a fully unified modern national movement without qualification is misleading.

Economic conditions

  • Land revenue pressures varied by region and tenure system.
  • Indian handicrafts faced intensifying competition from British manufactured goods, though the effects differed across crafts and regions.
  • The first passenger railway in India ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853; telegraph and postal systems expanded.
  • Infrastructure supported public communication but also troop movement, export trade and colonial administration.

Prominent national leaders across the Viceroy era

Early constitutional nationalism: Dadabhai Naoroji, W.C. Bonnerjee, Surendranath Banerjea, Pherozeshah Mehta and Gopal Krishna Gokhale. Swadeshi and assertive nationalism: Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo Ghosh and Rabindranath Tagore. Mass politics: Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel, C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Maulana Azad, Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Ali brothers. Revolutionary currents: Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, Surya Sen, Khudiram Bose and others. Social justice and constitutional politics: B.R. Ambedkar, Periyar and many regional leaders. INA and wartime nationalism: Subhas Chandra Bose and INA officers.

Colonial economy: balanced overview

  • Revenue and agriculture: land-revenue systems differed across regions. High or inflexible demands could intensify vulnerability, but the effects varied with harvests, local power relations, prices and tenure.
  • Manufacturing and trade: British manufactured imports and changing global trade harmed some Indian handicraft sectors; other sectors adapted or expanded. Deindustrialisation was uneven, not identical in every region or craft.
  • Railways, ports and telegraph: expanded connectivity and sometimes created new economic opportunities, but also served military mobility, export commerce and colonial extraction. Financing and returns often favoured British investors.
  • Famines: drought and crop failure interacted with purchasing power, market access, state relief policy, transport and administrative decisions. Bengal (1770), 1876–78 and Bengal (1943) must be studied in their specific contexts.
  • Nationalist economic critique: Dadabhai Naoroji’s “drain theory” and R.C. Dutt’s economic histories argued that colonial structures transferred resources and constrained Indian development. Their work was foundational; precise quantification remains debated.
  • War and depression: World War I brought inflation and fiscal pressure; the Great Depression damaged agricultural prices and exports; World War II brought shortages, inflation and severe hardship.
Exam caution: Do not infer that a ruler “caused” every event occurring during the tenure. Some events had long-term causes, involved multiple administrations, or occurred outside a ruler’s exact dates. Use “during the tenure” unless direct responsibility is demonstrable.

Quick revision: one-line facts

  • First Governor of Bengal after Plassey: Robert Clive.
  • First Governor-General of Bengal: Warren Hastings.
  • First Governor-General of India: Lord William Bentinck.
  • First Viceroy: Lord Canning.
  • Permanent Settlement: Cornwallis, 1793.
  • Subsidiary Alliance: associated with Wellesley.
  • Doctrine of Lapse: associated with Dalhousie; Awadh was annexed on alleged misgovernment, not by lapse.
  • Sati abolition regulation: 1829, under Bentinck.
  • Vernacular Press Act: Lytton, 1878; repealed under Ripon.
  • Local self-government resolution: Ripon, 1882.
  • Indian National Congress founded: 1885, during Dufferin’s tenure.
  • Partition of Bengal: 1905, Curzon; annulled in 1911, Hardinge II.
  • Jallianwala Bagh: 1919, Chelmsford.
  • Dandi March: 1930, Irwin.
  • Quit India: 1942, Linlithgow.
  • Last Viceroy: Mountbatten; first Indian Governor-General: C. Rajagopalachari.
